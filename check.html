Is this the right tool for me? Three quick questions

  1. Does your monthly mortgage payment include money for property taxes or home insurance?

    If yes, you have an escrow account and this page can check it. If you pay those bills yourself, there is no escrow account and nothing here to check.

  2. Is it a regular home mortgage from a bank, credit union, or mortgage company?

    The federal escrow rule covers most of these loans. It may not cover a home equity line of credit (HELOC), a loan from the seller or a relative, or some loans a small lender keeps for itself. If you are not sure, ask your servicer: “Is my loan a federally related mortgage loan?” If it is not, these results may not apply to you.

  3. Do you have your yearly escrow statement in front of you?

    It is often titled “Annual Escrow Account Disclosure Statement” or “Escrow Analysis”. Your servicer must send one every year. If you cannot find yours, ask them for a copy, or try an example above first.

    Step 1 of 4 Your payment optional

    Only the escrow part, not your whole mortgage payment. The whole payment also has principal and interest in it, and is a bigger number. Type dollar amounts like 1,234.56 or 1234.56. Commas are fine, and no dollar sign is needed.

    What you pay into escrow each month now. Also called “Escrow Deposit”, “Current Escrow Payment”, or the escrow line under “Current Monthly Mortgage Payment”.

    What the statement says it is changing to, each month. Also called “New Escrow Payment”, or the escrow line under “New Monthly Mortgage Payment”. If a “Shortage Installment” is listed separately, type the total with it added in.

    Step 2 of 4 Your balance

    The balance the statement’s forecast starts from. Also called “Beginning Balance”, “Anticipated Beginning Balance”, or “Starting Balance”.

    The first month in the statement’s forecast table. It is usually the month the new payment starts (“Effective…”). Often the next 12 months do not start in January.

    This page guessed this month. Check it against your statement.

    The cushion: a safety buffer your servicer says must stay in the account. Also called “Minimum Balance”, “Target Balance”, “Cushion”, or “Two-Month Reserve”. Sometimes it is only highlighted in the forecast table, not named. This is not the “lowest projected balance” line. That line is how low your balance is expected to drop. This box is the floor your servicer wants to keep under it. If your statement only shows a lowest projected balance, leave this box empty.

    Step 3 of 4 What the statement concluded optional

    A shortage means the account has money, but less than it should. A surplus (some servicers say “overage”) means it has more than it needs. A deficiency means the balance is below zero.

    Look in the summary near the top. Also called “Escrow Shortage”, “Surplus”, “Overage”, or “Deficiency”.

    Copy the amount straight from the statement’s summary. Also called “Shortage Amount” or “Surplus Amount”.

    How many monthly payments the statement spreads the repayment over. Most use 12. Also called “Shortage Installment” or “Shortage Spread”.

    7 Does the statement offer a “pay the shortage in full” option?

    Often a tear-off coupon, or a line such as “Option 1: pay the full shortage now”.

    The date of the analysis. It is usually printed near the top of the statement, though the rule does not require it. Also called “Analysis Date” or “Statement Date”. It is only used to work out the 30-day date when the rule calls for a refund.

    Step 4 of 4 9 Your bills for the next 12 months required

    Every bill your servicer plans to pay out of escrow in the next 12 months, and the month it gets paid. Also called “Expected Escrow Payments”, “Anticipated Disbursements”, or “What We Expect to Pay Out”.

    • Type each bill’s full amount, not a monthly share.
    • If property tax is paid twice a year, add it twice: once for each month.
    • Check for flood insurance and mortgage insurance (PMI or MIP). They are easy to miss.

    Less common situations

    Most people can skip this.

    The federal limit is 2 months of escrow payments, and most loans use it. Pick a smaller number only if your mortgage documents or your state’s law set a smaller cushion.

    Read all five limits
    • This is math, not legal advice. It does not tell you whether you have a legal claim, and it is not a lawyer or a substitute for one.
    • It is only as good as the numbers typed in. It cannot see your real bills or your servicer’s records. It can only redo the math from what you enter.
    • Your servicer may know something you don’t. A gap between your statement and the federal math can come from a newer tax bill or insurance premium than the one you typed. A mismatch is a question to ask, not proof of a mistake.
    • It only covers escrow. It checks the escrow rule (12 CFR 1024.17) and nothing else about your loan: not your interest rate, not whether mortgage insurance can come off.
    • Not every loan is covered by this rule. Some loans, like home equity lines of credit and some seller-financed loans, fall outside it. See “Is this the right tool for me?” near the top of the page.
