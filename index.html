Your escrow payment went up. Is the math right?

Escrow is the part of your mortgage payment that your servicer (the company you send the payment to) sets aside to pay your property taxes and insurance. A federal rule limits how much they may collect, and once a year they must send you a statement showing their math.

Many payment jumps are lawful: tax or insurance bills went up. Some statements have mistakes. This page tells you which one you are holding, in about two minutes.

Is this the right tool for me? Three quick questions

  1. Does your monthly mortgage payment include money for property taxes or home insurance?

    If yes, you have an escrow account and this page can check it. If you pay those bills yourself, there is no escrow account and nothing here to check.

  2. Is it a regular home mortgage from a bank, credit union, or mortgage company?

    The federal escrow rule covers most of these loans. It may not cover a home equity line of credit (HELOC), a loan from the seller or a relative, or some loans a small lender keeps for itself. If you are not sure, ask your servicer: “Is my loan a federally related mortgage loan?” If it is not, these results may not apply to you.

  3. Do you have your yearly escrow statement in front of you?

    It is often titled “Annual Escrow Account Disclosure Statement” or “Escrow Analysis”. Your servicer must send one every year. If you cannot find yours, ask them for a copy, or try an example above first.

Your statement

Type the numbers from your statement

The boxes follow the order most statements use, top to bottom. Two parts are required. Everything else is optional, and the more you fill in, the more this page can check. Type dollar amounts like 1,234.56 or 1234.56. Commas are fine, and no dollar sign is needed.

    Words on your statement, in plain English
    Escrow
    A savings-like account your mortgage company holds for you. It is used only to pay your property taxes and insurance when they are due.
    Servicer
    The company that collects your mortgage payment and runs your escrow account. It is not always the company that first gave you the loan.
    Escrow analysis
    The yearly checkup your servicer does on the account: did they collect the right amount last year, and how much should they collect next year?
    Disbursement
    A payment your servicer makes out of your escrow account, like a property tax bill or an insurance premium.
    Cushion
    A safety buffer your servicer may keep in the account. Federal law caps it at one sixth of your yearly bills, which is the same as 2 months of escrow payments.
    Low point
    The lowest your escrow balance is expected to drop to in the next 12 months.
    Shortage
    The account has money in it, but not as much as it is supposed to have.
    Deficiency
    The account went below zero. The servicer paid a bill with money that was not there yet.
    Surplus
    The account has more money in it than it needs. Some servicers call this an “overage”.
    Computation year
    The 12-month cycle your servicer uses to review the account. It often starts on the month of your first payment, not in January.
    RESPA and Regulation X
    RESPA is the federal law that sets the rules for mortgage escrow accounts. Regulation X is the detailed rule written under it. The escrow section is “12 CFR 1024.17”.
    CFPB
    The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau: the federal agency that writes these rules and takes complaints from consumers.
    Notice of error
    A letter you send your servicer asking them to look at a specific mistake. They have set deadlines to answer it.
    Request for information
    A letter asking your servicer for the worksheet behind your statement, or for an explanation of a number. It does not say anything is wrong. They generally have set deadlines to answer it.
    HUD-approved housing counselor
    A trained advisor, approved by the Department of Housing and Urban Development, who gives free or low-cost independent help with your mortgage.

    The fine print, up front

    What this page can’t tell you

    • This is math, not legal advice. It does not tell you whether you have a legal claim, and it is not a lawyer or a substitute for one.
    • It is only as good as the numbers typed in. It cannot see your real bills or your servicer’s records. It can only redo the math from what you enter.
    • Your servicer may know something you don’t. A gap between your statement and the federal math can come from a newer tax bill or insurance premium than the one you typed. A mismatch is a question to ask, not proof of a mistake.
    • It only covers escrow. It checks the escrow rule (12 CFR 1024.17) and nothing else about your loan: not your interest rate, not whether mortgage insurance can come off.
    • Not every loan is covered by this rule. Some loans, like home equity lines of credit and some seller-financed loans, fall outside it. See “Is this the right tool for me?” near the top of the page.
    How it works

    The federal method, run on your numbers.

    Federal rule 12 CFR 1024.17 tells every mortgage servicer how to run an escrow account: add up the year’s bills, divide by 12, walk the balance forward month by month, and keep a cushion no bigger than one sixth of the year’s bills. This page runs that same method on the numbers you type and compares the answer with your statement.

    The whole thing is a handful of plain files that run in your browser. There is no account and no tracking. The files are hosted on GitHub Pages, which, like any web host, can see that your browser downloaded them. It never sees what you type.
